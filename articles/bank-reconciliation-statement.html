Bank Reconciliation Statement (BRS): A Practical Guide for Accountants

A Bank Reconciliation Statement (BRS) explains the difference between the bank balance shown in a company’s cash book and the balance shown in its bank statement on a particular date. It helps accountants verify records, find missing entries and identify errors before closing the books.

For day-to-day accounting, bank reconciliation is more than a month-end task: it is an important control for cash, payments and receipts.

1. Why is bank reconciliation important?

  • It helps detect missed entries, duplicate postings and data-entry errors.
  • It identifies bank charges, interest and direct bank transactions not yet recorded in the books.
  • It helps track issued cheques or transfers that have not yet cleared.
  • It supports accurate cash reporting and strengthens fraud-prevention controls.

2. Common reasons for differences

Balances may differ because transactions are recorded at different times. Common examples include:

  • Cheques issued but not yet presented: The business has recorded a payment, but the bank has not processed it yet.
  • Deposits in transit: The business has recorded a receipt, but it has not appeared in the bank statement by the reconciliation date.
  • Bank charges or interest: The bank records these directly; the accountant may not have posted them yet.
  • Direct credits or debits: Examples include customer receipts, loan instalments or standing instructions.
  • Errors: A transaction may have been entered with the wrong amount or recorded twice in either set of records.

3. How to prepare a BRS: 5 practical steps

  1. Collect the records. Download the bank statement and open the cash book or bank ledger for the same period.
  2. Match transactions. Compare dates, references, amounts, receipts, payments and bank charges line by line.
  3. Mark matched items. Tick or mark entries appearing in both records. Keep unmatched items in a separate list.
  4. Investigate differences. Check clearing dates, bank advice, payment confirmations and supporting documents. Do not assume every difference is an error.
  5. Post approved book entries and prepare the statement. Record genuine missing items in the books after review, then document timing differences that remain outstanding.

4. Simple example

Suppose the cash book shows a bank balance of ₹50,000. A cheque for ₹8,000 has been issued and recorded in the cash book, but it has not yet been presented to the bank. If starting with the cash-book balance and reconciling to the bank statement, the bank statement balance would be ₹8,000 higher, assuming there are no other differences.

Important: The adjustment direction depends on whether you start with the cash-book balance or the bank-statement balance, and whether the account is in debit or overdraft. Always label the starting balance and verify the logic.

5. What should a BRS working sheet contain?

  • Reconciliation date and bank account name
  • Balance as per cash book and balance as per bank statement
  • Transaction date, description, reference number and amount
  • Matched/unmatched status and reason for the difference
  • Action owner, follow-up date and final resolution
  • Prepared-by and reviewed-by details, where applicable

6. Common mistakes to avoid

  • Reconciling different date ranges or using an incomplete statement.
  • Posting an adjustment before checking the bank advice or supporting document.
  • Leaving old unmatched items without an owner or follow-up date.
  • Forcing the balances to match through an unsupported journal entry.

Final checklist

Before closing the reconciliation, confirm that the opening balances are correct, all statement lines have been reviewed, book entries are approved, old outstanding items are explained, and the final BRS has been saved for review.

Consistent bank reconciliation improves cash visibility and helps keep accounting records reliable. The exact process may vary by organisation and accounting system, so follow your company’s approval and documentation policies.
